# Reusable Brand Naming & Identity Framework

**Document type:** Reusable brand strategy, naming and identity framework  
**Status:** Foundational framework — naming exploration is ongoing  
**Purpose:** Provide a reusable set of principles, criteria and processes for developing and evaluating a long-term brand name and identity.

---

## 1. Why this document exists

This document is not tied to a particular company, product, service, industry or market. It is a reusable framework for defining **how to think when choosing a name for a brand**.

A brand may begin with one offering, audience or area of activity, but its name should ideally have enough identity and flexibility to remain relevant as the organization, portfolio, audience or ambition evolves.

The goal is not to find a word that simply sounds modern, clever or technological. The goal is to find a name that can become a recognizable identity: something people can hear, say, remember, associate with an idea and eventually trust.

This document should act as the reference point for future naming discussions. New candidates should be judged against the principles here rather than generated as disconnected lists of attractive-sounding words.

---

## 2. The central idea

> **We are not just choosing a label. We are creating the foundation of a brand identity.**

A strong brand name should work on several levels:

1. **Sound:** It should sound good when spoken.
2. **Memory:** It should be easy to recognize and recall.
3. **Distinctiveness:** It should have its own character and not blend into a category of generic names.
4. **Meaning:** It should have a meaningful idea, association, philosophy or story behind it.
5. **Expression:** It should provide room for a slogan, brand narrative and identity.
6. **Flexibility:** It should not restrict the brand to one feature, offering or narrow market.
7. **Practicality:** It should be pronounceable, spellable, searchable and usable in real situations.
8. **Ownership:** It should have a realistic path to domain, trademark and commercial use.

The best name is not necessarily the cleverest, shortest, strangest or most meaningful name. It is the name that balances these qualities.

---

## 3. The kind of name we are looking for

The desired name should be:

- Catchy, without sounding childish or gimmicky.
- Distinctive, without being awkward or unnecessarily strange.
- Easy and satisfying to pronounce.
- Memorable after hearing or seeing it.
- Meaningful, with a philosophy, story or conceptual foundation.
- Capable of supporting a slogan or a wider brand message.
- Suitable for a serious, long-term business.
- Flexible enough to represent more than its initial offering.
- Appropriate for a brand that may serve different audiences and markets over time.
- Aligned in tone and emotional register with the intended brand personality (warm, precise, bold, calm, technical, approachable, etc.).

We want a name that creates a small but recognizable impression in someone's mind.

It should have some character. It should not feel like a generic label assembled from popular industry words.

### The desired balance

The target is not:

- So familiar that it feels ordinary.
- So abstract that it feels meaningless.
- So unusual that people cannot pronounce it.
- So descriptive that it limits future growth.
- So clever that the explanation is more memorable than the name itself.

The target is **distinctive but accessible, meaningful but natural, and memorable without trying too hard**.

Soft preference (not a rigid rule): most highly usable names land in the range of 1–3 syllables and roughly 5–10 letters. Longer names can work if the rhythm is strong and the spelling remains practical.

---

## 4. The thinking behind memorability

A name is experienced through more than its written form. People hear it, pronounce it, read it, repeat it, type it and associate it with experiences.

The following principles should guide our decisions.

### 4.1 Phonetic appeal — how the name sounds

The name should be enjoyable and natural to say.

Consider:

- The flow of vowels and consonants.
- Whether the sounds are clear or easily confused.
- Whether the name has a natural rhythm.
- Whether it feels comfortable when repeated.
- Whether the ending sounds complete and satisfying.
- Whether the name becomes awkward when spoken in a sentence.

A name should be evaluated aloud, not only by looking at it in uppercase letters.

### 4.2 Pronunciation simplicity

A distinctive name is useful only if people can reasonably say it.

We should test:

- Can someone pronounce it on first sight?
- Do different people arrive at roughly the same pronunciation?
- Can someone repeat it after hearing it once?
- Can someone spell it after hearing it?
- Does it work across likely accents and languages?

The name does not need to be pronounced identically by everyone, but it should not create avoidable confusion.

### 4.3 Distinctiveness and recognition

A name should have a recognizable identity.

Distinctiveness can come from:

- An uncommon but natural sound pattern.
- A surprising yet understandable word.
- A memorable combination of syllables.
- A meaningful association.
- A fresh interpretation of a familiar concept.

Being different is not enough by itself. The name should be different in a way that is easy to process and remember.

### 4.4 Cognitive ease

People tend to process familiar patterns more easily than confusing ones. However, a name that is too familiar may also feel generic.

We therefore want a useful balance:

- Familiar enough to pronounce.
- Unusual enough to notice.
- Simple enough to remember.
- Interesting enough to invite curiosity.

This is a practical naming principle, not a guarantee of recall. Audience testing is needed to understand how a particular name performs.

### 4.5 Sound symbolism

Sounds can create impressions even when people do not consciously analyze them.

For example, some sharper consonant sounds may feel energetic or technical, while softer sounds may feel more fluid or approachable. These associations vary across people, languages and contexts.

We should use sound symbolism as a creative consideration, not as a universal psychological rule.

### 4.6 Visual identity

The written name matters too.

Consider:

- How it looks in uppercase and lowercase.
- Whether its letter combination looks balanced.
- Whether it can work in a logo.
- Whether it remains readable at small sizes and in compact digital placements.
- Whether it looks credible on a website, presentation or business document.
- Whether it is easy to type and search.

A name should work as both a spoken identity and a visual identity.

## 5. Meaning, philosophy and story

A name should ideally have something behind it.

That meaning can come from several sources.

### 5.1 A real word with an existing meaning

A real word may already carry an idea, emotion or association.

**Advantages:**

- It can be easier to explain.
- It may already be familiar.
- It can create an immediate conceptual connection.
- It can provide a natural foundation for a brand story.

**Risks:**

- It may be widely used.
- It may be difficult to protect legally.
- Its existing meaning may not fit the brand.
- It may be difficult to find a suitable domain.

### 5.2 An invented or coined word

A coined name can be designed around a particular sound, rhythm and identity.

**Advantages:**

- It can be more distinctive.
- Its meaning can be developed by the brand.
- It may offer more flexibility.
- It can become strongly associated with one business over time.

**Risks:**

- People may not know how to pronounce it.
- It may initially have no meaning.
- Its spelling may be difficult to remember.
- It can sound artificial if it is constructed without care.

An invented name should still feel intentional, pronounceable and natural.

### 5.3 A meaningful acronym or expansion

One of the naming directions we particularly want to explore is a name that can also have a meaningful letter-by-letter expansion.

This creates two layers:

- The name itself works as a memorable brand.
- The expansion reveals a deeper idea or philosophy.

For example, a word might represent a set of principles, values or capabilities that define the brand.

However, the expansion must not be forced.

**A good name should work even before someone knows its expansion.** The expansion should enrich the name, not rescue it.

### 5.4 A conceptual or symbolic name

A name can also be inspired by a larger idea:

- Connection
- Progress
- Intelligence
- Discovery
- Transformation
- Possibility
- Unity
- Clarity
- Momentum
- Excellence
- Evolution
- A complete ecosystem

The word does not need to describe the business literally. It can represent the philosophy behind it.

### 5.5 Brand personality and emotional register

Beyond conceptual meaning, the name should feel consistent with the intended personality of the brand. Ask:

- Does the sound and character of the name feel warm, precise, bold, calm, technical, approachable, or whatever tone the brand requires?
- Does it create the emotional impression we want people to carry?

This is often a quiet but decisive factor in whether a name feels “right.”

### 5.6 A brand story

A strong name should allow us to answer:

- Why does this name exist?
- What does it represent?
- What belief or idea does it communicate?
- Why is it appropriate for this brand?
- What should people associate with it?

The story should be simple enough to explain naturally.

We should avoid elaborate explanations that require people to remember a paragraph just to understand the name.

---

## 6. The role of slogans and brand expression

The name should create room for a larger identity.

A good name can become the foundation for:

- A slogan or tagline.
- A brand philosophy.
- A one-sentence positioning statement.
- A company introduction.
- An offering-family naming system.
- A visual identity and logo.
- A consistent marketing voice.

The slogan should complement the name rather than repeat it.

For example, a name might represent unity or connection, while the slogan expresses the practical promise of bringing things together.

The exact slogan should be developed after the strongest name candidates emerge. It should not be used to compensate for a weak name.

---

## 7. Brand ambition and flexibility

The name should be considered from a long-term perspective.

It may begin with one offering, but the organization could eventually offer additional products, services, modules or solutions.

Therefore, we should ask:

- Does the name still work if the offering expands?
- Does it sound like a brand or merely a feature, function or offering?
- Can it represent an organization as well as an offering?
- Can it support a family of related offerings, services or initiatives?
- Will it still feel appropriate several years from now?
- Does it depend too heavily on a current technology trend?
- Does it restrict the business to one industry or audience?

A broad name is not automatically better. It still needs a recognizable identity. The objective is to preserve room for growth without becoming vague or meaningless.

---

## 8. Visual identity and logo potential

Naming should not be treated as completely separate from visual identity. The name and the visual identity are connected parts of the same brand. The name should offer inspiration for a distinctive visual direction, while the identity should reinforce the name's meaning, personality and philosophy.

A name does not need to describe a literal object or have an obvious symbol. Its underlying idea may inspire an abstract mark, a distinctive wordmark, a lettermark or a combination of these. A name should not be selected only because it is easy to draw, but its visual potential should be considered before the final decision.

### 8.1 Logo directions to consider

1. **Wordmark:** A distinctive typographic treatment of the full name.
2. **Abstract symbol:** A unique symbol inspired by the brand's idea or philosophy rather than a literal illustration.
3. **Lettermark or monogram:** A recognizable treatment of one or more letters from the name.
4. **Symbol and wordmark:** A coordinated symbol alongside the full brand name.

These are creative directions to explore, not rules that every brand must follow. The strongest approach depends on the name and the identity we want to build.

### 8.2 Visual identity evaluation

For each serious name candidate, consider:

- **Symbol potential:** Can its meaning or philosophy inspire a distinctive visual mark?
- **Typography:** Do its letters offer possibilities for a recognizable wordmark?
- **Distinctiveness:** Can the visual direction avoid generic or category-cliché imagery?
- **Scalability:** Can the identity work at both large and very small sizes?
- **Versatility:** Does it work across digital and physical contexts, light and dark backgrounds, and colour and monochrome use?
- **Brand architecture:** Can it extend consistently across future offerings without losing the master brand's identity?
- **Coherence:** Do the name, symbol, typography, colours and wider visual language feel like parts of the same brand?

### 8.3 Test the identity in real contexts

A logo should not be judged only as a large presentation mockup. Test it where people will actually encounter the brand, such as:

- Website headers and landing pages.
- Product or service interfaces and navigation areas.
- Mobile application icons, favicons and other compact placements.
- Presentations, proposals, reports and official documents.
- Signage, packaging or other physical applications, where relevant.
- Social profiles and other digital communication.
- Email signatures and UI micro-contexts.

Also test a symbol without the full name, horizontal and stacked arrangements, monochrome versions, and use on light and dark backgrounds. The identity should remain clear and recognizable without depending on complex effects or a single colour treatment.

### 8.4 The relationship between name and identity

The visual identity should not merely decorate the name. It should help make the name recognizable and reinforce the idea behind it. Equally, the name should give the identity a meaningful foundation.

> **The name should inspire the identity. The identity should reinforce the name. And both should represent a brand that can grow beyond its first offering.**

The objective is not simply to create an attractive logo. It is to build a coherent identity system that can be recognized, applied consistently and developed over time.

## 9. Naming approaches to explore

We should explore multiple creative territories instead of repeatedly generating variations of one naming pattern.

### Territory A — Meaningful real words

Words with strong associations, memorable sounds and a natural story.

Potential inspiration: discovery, unity, progress, clarity, intelligence, momentum and transformation.

### Territory B — Invented but pronounceable words

New words deliberately designed around sound, rhythm, spelling and visual appeal.

The goal is to create something original without making it difficult to say or remember.

### Territory C — Hybrid names

A name that sounds like a natural word or brand but has a deeper, deliberately developed meaning.

This may be especially promising because it balances memorability with brand storytelling.

### Territory D — Acronym-based names

A short, pronounceable name whose letters can represent a meaningful phrase or set of principles.

The expansion must sound natural and communicate a coherent idea.

### Territory E — Symbolic and metaphorical names

Names inspired by a larger concept, image or philosophy rather than a literal product description.

This approach can create an identity that is emotionally and conceptually richer than a purely descriptive name.

These territories should be explored independently before comparing candidates. Generate volume early across territories, then filter rigorously to a focused set of serious candidates.

## 10. What we should avoid

The following are important negative constraints.

### 10.1 Generic construction

Avoid predictable formulas such as:

- Industry keyword + random suffix.
- Generic technology term + generic noun.
- A familiar prefix followed by an arbitrary invented ending.

A category-related word can be useful, but it should not be the only source of identity.

### 10.2 Random futuristic naming

A word does not become a strong brand simply because it contains unusual letters or looks technological.

Avoid names that sound like interchangeable startup names.

### 10.3 Forced acronyms

Do not choose a word first and then assemble unnatural phrases to fit every letter.

If the expansion sounds awkward, revise it or use a different naming approach.

### 10.4 Difficult spelling or pronunciation

Avoid unnecessary silent letters, ambiguous vowel combinations, confusing consonant clusters and inconsistent pronunciation.

### 10.5 Excessive complexity

A long name may have an interesting story, but it can be harder to use in conversation, logos, domains and digital interfaces.

### 10.6 Overly narrow meaning

Avoid names that make the brand sound limited to one feature, function, offering or temporary category unless that restriction is intentional.

### 10.7 Trend dependence

Avoid relying entirely on fashionable terms that may become dated.

A long-term brand should not need to change its identity whenever technology vocabulary changes.

### 10.8 Unverified claims

Do not call a name unique, available or legally safe without checking.

Creative originality and legal availability are different questions.

### 10.9 Weak trademark strength

Be aware of the classic distinctiveness spectrum used in trademark law (especially helpful for understanding protectability):

- **Generic** — the common name for the goods/services (almost never protectable as a brand).
- **Descriptive** — directly describes a quality, feature or characteristic (weak protection; often requires secondary meaning).
- **Suggestive** — requires imagination or thought to connect to the offering (stronger protection).
- **Arbitrary** — a real word used in an unrelated context (very strong).
- **Fanciful / coined** — invented words with no prior meaning (strongest inherent protection).

Prefer names that sit further toward the suggestive / arbitrary / fanciful end of the spectrum when long-term ownership matters.

## 11. A better decision framework

We should not select a name based only on personal taste or how impressive it looks in a list.

Every serious candidate should be evaluated against a consistent framework.

| Criterion                         | Suggested weight | Main question                                                  |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sound and catchiness              | 15%              | Does it sound good and feel satisfying to say?                 |
| Memorability                      | 15%              | Is it easy to recognize and recall?                            |
| Pronunciation                     | 15%              | Can people say it correctly and naturally?                     |
| Distinctiveness                   | 15%              | Does it have its own recognizable identity?                    |
| Meaning, philosophy & personality | 10%              | Does it have a credible idea, story and fitting tone?          |
| Brand-story and slogan potential  | 10%              | Can it support a meaningful identity?                          |
| Flexibility and longevity         | 10%              | Can it grow with the business?                                 |
| Spelling, searchability & digital | 5%               | Can people find it after hearing it? Clean search real estate? |
| Commercial feasibility            | 5%               | Is there a realistic path to using and protecting it?          |

### Total: 100%

The weights are a starting point. They can be adjusted if we discover that a particular factor is more important.

**Important:** Commercial feasibility (domain, trademark risk, existing conflicts) should function primarily as a **pass/fail gate**, especially on the shortlist. A high creative score cannot compensate for a name that is realistically unownable. Preliminary conflict and domain checks should occur before heavy emotional or visual investment.

Scores should support discussion, not pretend to be scientific measurements.

### Mandatory rejection checks

Regardless of score, a candidate should be rejected or investigated further if:

- It has an offensive or unfortunate meaning in an important target language.
- Its pronunciation is seriously ambiguous.
- It is confusingly similar to a relevant existing brand.
- It has an obvious trademark conflict.
- Its spelling is impractical.
- It creates an unwanted association that cannot reasonably be corrected.
- It sits too far toward the generic or purely descriptive end of the trademark spectrum with no realistic path to protection.

## 12. How to test a name in the real world

A name that looks good in a document may not work in actual use.

The strongest candidates should go through practical tests.

### Test 1 — First-sight pronunciation

Show the written name to people without explaining it.

Ask them to pronounce it.

Observe whether they arrive at a reasonably consistent reading.

### Test 2 — Heard-name spelling

Say the name aloud without showing its spelling.

Ask people to write it down.

This reveals whether the name is easy to reconstruct after hearing it.

### Test 3 — Recall

Introduce a name among several other candidates.

After a short interval, ask which names people remember.

This gives a basic indication of recall, though a more controlled test would be needed for reliable conclusions.

### Test 4 — Sentence test

Use the name naturally in sentences:

- "Have you heard of \_\_\_?"
- "We use \_\_\_ for our operations."
- "I work at \_\_\_."
- "You can find us online at \_\_\_."

The name should feel natural rather than forced.

### Test 5 — Association test

Ask people what the name makes them think of.

Look for:

- Positive or negative associations.
- Confusing meanings.
- Unintended interpretations.
- Whether the name feels credible.
- Whether the name creates the intended impression and emotional register.

### Test 6 — Visual test

View the name in:

- Uppercase.
- Lowercase.
- A simple wordmark.
- A small icon.
- A website header.
- A presentation title.
- App icon / favicon contexts.

It should remain recognizable and visually usable.

### Test 7 — Brand-extension test

Put the name next to possible offering or portfolio labels.

Check whether the master brand can support a coherent family of offerings.

### Test 8 — Cross-cultural and linguistic test

Check pronunciation, spelling, meanings, homophones, slang and unwanted associations in relevant languages and markets.

Do not assume that a name that works in one language will work everywhere. For priority markets, consider professional linguistic screening.

## 13. Practical and legal validation

A name can be creatively excellent but commercially unusable.

Before final selection, investigate thoroughly. Preliminary checks should begin as soon as a shortlist forms.

### Domain availability

- Is a suitable domain available (ideally .com or the most relevant extension for the markets)?
- Is the spelling practical for a web address?
- Are confusingly similar domains already associated with another business?

### Trademark availability

- Search the relevant trademark databases (national and, where appropriate, international).
- Consider the markets in which the brand will operate.
- Consider the relevant goods and services classifications.
- Investigate similar-sounding and similar-looking marks, not only exact matches.
- Seek professional trademark advice before making a significant commercial commitment.

### Existing brand conflicts

- Search for companies and products with the same or similar names.
- Check relevant app stores, search engines and business directories.
- Look for confusingly similar brands in related industries.

### Social and digital identity

- Check relevant social handles.
- Consider how easily the name can be searched and whether it creates clean search real estate.
- Look for existing online associations or dominant competing uses.

**Important:** Domain availability does not establish trademark availability, and an exact-match search alone does not establish legal safety. Trademark clearance is a professional legal question.

## 15. Principles for a disciplined naming process

A disciplined process helps separate promising names from ideas that only sound appealing at first.

### Improvement 1 — Separate the name from its explanation

First ask whether the name itself is good.

Only then develop its expansion and story.

A strong expansion cannot compensate for a weak or awkward name.

### Improvement 2 — Do not overvalue acronyms

Acronyms are a useful way to add depth, but they are not the only way to create meaning.

A real word with a powerful story or a coined word with a coherent philosophy may be stronger.

### Improvement 3 — Test the sound before falling in love with the spelling

A name may look beautiful but sound awkward.

Pronunciation should be evaluated early, not after the shortlist is complete.

### Improvement 4 — Test both recognition and recall

A name can be distinctive without being memorable.

We should test whether people can recognize it again and recall it after hearing it.

### Improvement 5 — Avoid generating too many weak candidates

A smaller number of carefully developed candidates is more useful than a long list of random names.

Each candidate should have a reason to exist. Generate volume across territories first, then focus effort on the strongest options.

### Improvement 6 — Consider legal and digital ownership early

A promising name should receive an initial conflict and domain check before too much time is invested in its visual identity and marketing. Treat commercial feasibility as a gate, not a late afterthought.

### Improvement 7 — Distinguish personal preference from audience response

The founder's preference matters, but the name will eventually be used by customers, employees and partners.

A small amount of real-world feedback can reveal pronunciation or association problems that are not obvious to the person who created the name.

### Improvement 8 — Preserve flexibility without becoming vague

A broad brand name is useful, but it still needs a clear identity.

The brand should be flexible in what it can represent, not unclear about what it stands for.

## 16. Recommended naming workflow

The next naming exercise should follow a structured sequence.

### Stage 1 — Explore the creative territories

Generate ideas independently from:

- Real words.
- Invented words.
- Hybrid words.
- Acronyms.
- Symbolic and metaphorical concepts.

Aim for healthy volume across territories early, then begin filtering.

### Stage 2 — Create a focused candidate set

Develop approximately 20–30 serious candidates (drawn from a larger initial exploration if needed).

For each candidate, document:

- Name.
- Pronunciation.
- Syllable count / approximate length.
- Meaning or inspiration.
- Expansion, if applicable.
- Brand story and personality fit.
- Possible slogan.
- Strengths.
- Risks and possible confusion.

### Stage 3 — First screening

Remove names that are:

- Difficult to pronounce.
- Too generic or purely descriptive with weak protectability.
- Too similar to common competitors.
- Hard to spell.
- Meaningless without a long explanation.
- Too narrow for the intended brand ambition.
- Showing early domain or obvious conflict problems.

### Stage 4 — Shortlist

Select approximately five strong candidates using the evaluation framework. Run preliminary domain, social and trademark conflict checks at this stage.

### Stage 5 — Audience testing

Test pronunciation, spelling, recall, associations and emotional impression with a small group of people representing likely audiences.

### Stage 6 — Commercial validation

Investigate domains, trademarks, existing brands and digital identity more thoroughly. Seek professional advice as needed before final commitment.

### Stage 7 — Brand development

Only after selecting the name should we finalize:

- The official meaning or expansion.
- The brand philosophy.
- The main slogan.
- The positioning statement.
- The visual identity.
- Any offering or portfolio naming system.

## 17. Reusable creative brief for future naming sessions

The following brief can be copied into a future conversation or shared with a naming consultant.

> I want to create a distinctive, memorable and meaningful name for a long-term brand.
>
> I am not looking for a generic descriptive name or a random futuristic startup word. I want a name with its own character—something catchy, pleasant to pronounce, easy to remember and distinctive enough to create a recognizable impression.
>
> The name should ideally have a natural rhythm and be reasonably easy to pronounce and spell across relevant audiences and accents. Soft preference for 1–3 syllables and practical length.
>
> I want depth behind the name. It may be a real word, an invented word, a hybrid or a pronounceable acronym. Ideally, it should support a meaningful expansion, philosophy or story that can eventually become part of the brand identity and provide a foundation for a strong slogan.
>
> However, do not force an acronym or an artificial meaning. The name itself must be strong before its explanation is considered.
>
> The brand should be suitable for a serious long-term business and flexible enough to grow beyond its initial offering or category. The name’s tone should align with the desired brand personality.
>
> Think about how people hear, pronounce, process, remember, spell, search for and recommend a name. Consider phonetic appeal, cognitive ease, distinctiveness, visual identity, cultural interpretation, longevity, emotional register and commercial practicality.
>
> Explore meaningful real words, carefully invented names, hybrid concepts, symbolic names and acronym-based names as separate creative directions.
>
> Do not give me random lists. Develop each serious candidate with its pronunciation, meaning, expansion if appropriate, story, slogan potential, strengths and risks.
>
> Evaluate candidates using a consistent framework, test the strongest options in real-world contexts and identify domain and trademark questions that require validation early.
>
> The objective is not merely to name an initial offering. It is to establish the foundation of a memorable and recognizable brand.

---

## 18. Final guiding principles

When evaluating a candidate, return to these questions:

1. Does it sound good?
2. Is it easy to pronounce?
3. Is it easy to remember?
4. Does it stand apart?
5. Does it have a meaningful identity and fitting emotional tone?
6. Can its story be explained simply?
7. Can it support a slogan?
8. Can it grow beyond its initial category or offering?
9. Does it work across the audiences and markets that matter?
10. Can it realistically be used and protected as a brand?

If a name is attractive but fails several of these questions, we should not keep it merely because we like its sound.

---

## 19. The final philosophy

A name should not be a random word decorated with a slogan. It should be the beginning of an identity.

It should sound natural enough to be spoken, distinctive enough to be recognized, meaningful enough to be explained and flexible enough to grow.

The name should create the first impression. The offering and the experience should give that name its reputation.

> **We are not searching for a word that describes what we sell. We are searching for a name that can represent what we build, what we believe and what the brand may become.**

**Current status:** This is a reusable framework. Any specific naming project using it should record its candidates, decisions and validation results separately. No brand name is selected by this document.
